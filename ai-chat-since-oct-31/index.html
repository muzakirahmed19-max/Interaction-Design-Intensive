Work flow
What is a Workflow?
A workflow defines the logical sequence of actions to be performed to achieve a specific user goal. At this stage, a workflow does not imagine or define any visual UI elements (such as buttons, colors, or page layouts); rather, it focuses strictly on the underlying use cases, stages, and screens of a user's interaction.
Intent & Purpose Behind Making a Workflow
Creating a workflow before touching any visual design serves several strategic purposes:

  • Designing Experience Flows: Outlines how users navigate through stages to satisfy both user and business goals.
  • Measuring Design & Engineering Effort: Allows teams to estimate the effort required for a Minimum Viable Product (MVP) upfront by accurately counting unique screens and sub screens.
  • Mapping Product Complexity: Helps visualize complexity across stages and plan for both primary "happy paths" and alternative paths.
  • Establishing Direction Without Premature UI Design: Defines system functionality and screen goals early on, preventing designers from getting bogged down in premature visual styling (such as footers, colors, or component layouts).
  • Comprehensive Error & Edge-Case Planning: Ensures potential friction points and error states are pre-planned before building high-fidelity assets.

Instructional Steps to Build a Complete Workflow
Step 1: Establish the User Scenario & Define Goals

  • Starting Point: Begin with the consolidated user scenario produced during scenario mapping.
  • User Goal: Write a crisp goal from a designer's perspective defining what the user should achieve (e.g., helping a user easily book an affordable cab to reach their destination stress-free).
  • Business Goal: Write a clear business goal outlining the value to the organization from the perspective of a designer. (e.g., increasing ride conversion rates and building loyalty).

Step 2: Map the Logical Sequence & Naming (Unique vs. Subscreens)
Define the sequential flow of steps or screens using absolute and decimal numbering to help estimate design scope and effort:

  • Unique Screens (1.0, 2.0, 3.0): Major screens addressing a substantial goal that require significant design effort (e.g., 4 to 8 hours). Example: 2.0 Search Results.
  • Subscreens (1.1, 1.2, 2.1): Minor state changes or updates within a unique screen that do not alter the overall screen goal or require heavy effort. Example: 2.1 Filters Open and Applied.

Step 3: Connect Stages via Primary Call-to-Actions (CTAs)

  • Map navigation from step to step to show how users transition through the system.
  • Connection Rule: Connection arrows must always originate directly from a specific action button/CTA (e.g., Request Now) and point to the destination screen. Arrows should never start vaguely from the edge of a screen box.

Step 4: Define Screen Goals & Functional Descriptions
For every screen or stage in the workflow, document two elements:

  • Screen Goal: State clearly what the user should achieve on that specific screen (e.g., building credibility or enabling option comparison).
  • Screen Description: Detail what the user can do, what inputs are required, and what happens next.
  • Strict Constraint: Descriptions must specify functional capabilities and use cases only. Do not include UI design patterns (e.g., headers, footers, checkboxes) or UI specifications (e.g., colors, height, width).

Step 5: Identify and Plan Error States
Identify potential errors that can occur during any action or transition:

  • Error Numbering: Number errors sequentially as Error 1, Error 2, Error 3, regardless of whether they occur on a unique screen or subscreen.
  • Actionable Recovery: For every identified error, explicitly state what the user can do to recover (e.g., Error 1: Location not found → User can re-enter location, choose location on map, or retry).

Step 6: Evaluate Pros and Cons
Assess the completed workflow to evaluate its efficiency and spot potential friction points:

  • Critical Questions: Ask whether this is the most efficient path for the scenario, whether any loopholes exist, and where users might encounter problems.
  • Contextual Rationale: Frame pros and cons strictly around the defined scenario and goals. Avoid generic statements like "it's intuitive"; always provide the specific reasoning.

Variation A: People-first (search, then allocate)

Arjun starts from people and builds the team one person at a time.

  1. Views availability across teams for next week
  2. Searches by skill, seniority, hours and dates
  3. Opens a candidate's profile
  4. Allocates the person to Atlas with hours and dates
  5. The system checks for conflicts on save and flags the Orion overrun overlap
  6. Resolves the conflict by splitting hours, swapping the person or shifting dates
  7. Repeats steps 2–6 for the second developer
  8. Confirms, and the people and the other manager are notified

Pros

  • Matches how Arjun works today ("who's free?"), so it's easy to adopt
  • Gives full control over each person choice, which suits his familiarity-based judgement

Cons

  • Conflicts surface only after allocating, so he may pick someone and then have to undo it
  • Repeating the loop for every person is slow for a multi-person project, which works against the business goal of cutting staffing time
  • The Orion overrun is only discovered by accident, when someone happens to clash

Variation B: Project-first (demand, then system-suggested matches)

Arjun starts from the project's needs, and the system proposes the team.

  1. Opens the Atlas project
  2. Defines the need: 2 senior backend developers, 20 hrs/week, 6 weeks, from next Monday
  3. The system checks for existing clashes first and flags that Orion is overrunning and two of its developers are already booked on Atlas from tomorrow
  4. Resolves the Orion overrun: extends Orion and chooses who stays, with the knock-on effect shown
  5. The system suggests ranked matches for each open role, based on skills, free hours and conflict-free dates
  6. Reviews the matches and their profiles, and picks the people
  7. Reviews a summary of all allocations, with each person's weekly total and team utilization
  8. Confirms everything at once, and notifications go out

Pros

  • Conflicts are resolved before choosing people, so nothing is confirmed that would double-book someone, which directly meets the user goal
  • Suggestions only show conflict-free people, which cuts search time (business goal) and surfaces underused talent, not just familiar names (F5)
  • The summary before confirming gives one moment of confidence that nobody is overloaded

Cons

  • Depends on accurate skill and availability data; bad data gives bad suggestions
  • Arjun may distrust system suggestions at first and want to search manually
  • Defining the need first adds a step when he already knows exactly who he wants

Variation C: Schedule-first (fix the team timeline, then fill gaps)

Arjun starts from his team's schedule and treats staffing as rebalancing.

  1. Views the team schedule for the next 6 weeks, with overloads and overlaps highlighted
  2. Spots the Orion overrun colliding with the Atlas start
  3. Adjusts Orion allocations directly on the schedule
  4. Identifies the remaining gaps for Atlas
  5. Fills each gap from his team, or searches other teams if nobody is free
  6. Rechecks utilization and confirms

Pros

  • Gives the best overview of workload and overruns, which suits a manager who thinks in weeks
  • Ongoing rebalancing and new staffing happen in one place

Cons

  • Starts from his own team only, so it repeats the siloed view (F1) and misses people in other teams unless he searches separately
  • Heavy for a simple "staff 2 people" task, and the schedule gets crowded with 10–12 concurrent projects
  • Relies on Arjun to spot the problems, rather than the system catching them

Converge

Recommendation: Variation B (project-first) is the base, with Variation A's manual search as an alternative path when Arjun already knows who he wants or distrusts the suggestions. B is the only variation that catches the Orion overrun clash before any allocation, shows conflict-free people only and confirms everything in one place. That serves both the user goal (staffing with confidence, no overload) and the business goal (faster staffing, zero double-booking).
